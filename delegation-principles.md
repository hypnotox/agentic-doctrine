# Delegation principles

These principles specialize the [general principles](general-principles.md) for handing work to other agents, including subagents and parallel sessions. Apply them when deciding whether to delegate, briefing a delegate, and relying on its results.

## 1. Delegate for a concrete benefit.

Delegate when a separate context provides independence, keeps bulky investigation out of the main context, or lets independent work proceed in parallel. Keep work inline when the handoff would cost more than it saves or the work depends on context that cannot be conveyed efficiently.

## 2. Give each delegate a self-contained brief.

A delegate starts without the conversation's context. State the intended outcome, settled decisions and constraints, what it may read or change, and how its result will be judged. Leave the method open unless the method is itself settled. A delegate acts within the delegating agent's authority, never beyond it.

## 3. Keep independent review independent.

Use fresh context for reviews and second opinions so the reviewer judges the work rather than inheriting the author's reasoning. Brief it with the purpose, evidence, and criteria, not the conclusion it should confirm.

## 4. Match capability to the work.

Choose model capability and reasoning effort according to the judgment the work requires, the concerns that must be understood together, and the cost of errors that verification would not catch.

## 5. Own the combined result.

The delegating agent remains responsible for the outcome. Treat a delegate's report as claims to evaluate rather than established fact: check it against the brief and the evidence before relying on it, reconcile conflicting findings, and verify the integrated result.
