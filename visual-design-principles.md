# Visual design principles

These principles specialize the [general principles](general-principles.md) for painting, illustration, graphic design, interfaces, and 3D work. Apply them according to the intended experience and medium. The [typography principles](typography-principles.md) own text-specific guidance; the [animation principles](animation-principles.md) own movement over time.

## 1. Start with the intended experience.

Establish what the work should communicate, evoke, or invite the viewer to notice. Let that intent guide visual choices. Where references inform the work, distinguish their defining qualities from interpretations and deliberate departures. Clarity, ambiguity, abstraction, and distortion can each serve a purpose; realism and physical plausibility are requirements only when the brief calls for them.

## 2. Compose the whole before refining its parts.

Arrange the major shapes, masses, spaces, and relationships so the whole supports the intent. Consider balance, rhythm, scale, and negative space before committing to small details. A strong isolated element still needs to work within its composition.

## 3. Establish form and proportion deliberately.

Resolve silhouettes, primary forms, and the proportions between them before refinement. Preserve the identity or relationships the work needs to convey. Judge stylization and exaggeration against the intended effect rather than a universal model of correct anatomy or construction.

## 4. Use value and color to shape perception.

Use relationships in value, hue, saturation, and contrast to establish mood, separate or connect forms, and direct attention. Judge colors together and against their surroundings. Distinction matters where the viewer needs to perceive a difference; deliberate merging or ambiguity can serve other purposes.

## 5. Give attention a deliberate hierarchy.

Decide what should attract attention first and how the eye should move through the work. Coordinate scale, position, contrast, edges, and detail around those priorities. Let supporting elements contribute without competing accidentally with the focus.

## 6. Keep the visual language coherent.

Make choices about shapes, marks, edges, color, and detail relate across the work. Consistency should support its identity while leaving room for meaningful variation and contrast. Related pieces should belong together without losing the distinctions their purposes require.

## 7. Make detail serve the work.

Use detail to strengthen form, identity, mood, narrative, texture, or expression. Judge its contribution to the whole rather than treating more detail as better. Functional explanations and visible construction belong where the intended subject or brief requires them.

## 8. Test major choices with rough studies.

Explore consequential visual choices before costly refinement. Use proportionate studies, such as sketches or blockouts, to compare composition, form, and color relationships. Resolve the important uncertainties before polish makes a weak direction expensive to change.

## 9. Judge the actual output in its intended context.

Inspect the delivered work at the scale, viewpoints, and surroundings in which it will be experienced. Check whether its composition, identity, and intended effect survive presentation and integration. Attractive isolated previews and correct production settings do not establish that result.
