# Animation principles

These principles specialize the [general principles](general-principles.md) for 2D and 3D animation, interface transitions, and other animated output. Apply the [visual design principles](visual-design-principles.md) to its visual presentation.

## 1. Make motion serve the intended reading.

Establish what the action, expression, or state change should communicate. Let that purpose guide how the motion unfolds and what the viewer needs to notice.

## 2. Stage motion around the intended focus.

Use poses, silhouettes, framing, and staging to guide what the viewer notices. Preserve required visual cues and subject-defining features where recognition matters. Clarity, ambiguity, and expressive distortion can each serve the intended experience.

## 3. Shape timing, spacing, and rhythm deliberately.

Judge how movement develops over time: the pace of an action, its holds, acceleration and deceleration, and the paths between positions. Use those relationships to communicate the intended energy, emphasis, and character.

## 4. Give movement coherent dynamics.

Where weight, momentum, support, or material behavior matter, make them legible through the coordinated motion of the parts. Judge contacts and changes of support across the action. Believable movement can be stylized; deliberate departures should serve the brief rather than excuse an unintended failure.

## 5. Carry the action through transitions.

Follow the motion through intervening positions and relevant starts, stops, loops, blends, and interruptions. Preserve the intended relationships and cues as actions connect. Look for unintended snaps, sliding, collisions, and seams that disrupt the result.

## 6. Resolve consequential motion questions before polishing.

Use rough studies where they help resolve consequential questions about poses, timing, and transitions early. When an approach fails to produce the intended motion, reconsider its construction before layering on corrections or detail. Refine what already reads convincingly.

## 7. Judge motion by actually watching it.

View continuous playback at the intended speed and in the context where the animation will be seen, including relevant cycles and transitions. Stills, sampled poses, and slow playback help diagnose problems but cannot establish timing or continuity. Quantitative checks can protect real constraints; they do not establish motion quality. If continuous playback has not been viewed, leave that quality unassessed.
